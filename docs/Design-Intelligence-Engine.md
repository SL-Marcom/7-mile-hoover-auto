# Design Intelligence Engine

## Purpose

This document defines how SiteLab should design premium, custom-looking websites for local businesses without falling back to generic template patterns.

The goal is to create work that feels original, intentional, and appropriate for the client’s real context while still remaining practical, scalable, and production-ready.

## Core principle

Every website must feel custom-made.

That means:
- no exact layout reuse between clients,
- no template-first design decisions,
- no visual pattern copying without transformation,
- and no generic AI-style compositions made from repeated rounded cards, decorative gradients, or interchangeable sections.

## Design philosophy

SiteLab should design by understanding intent, not by copying surface style.

### Design should be driven by:
- the client’s business reality,
- the site’s primary conversion goal,
- the audience’s expectations,
- the local context,
- and the brand’s real constraints.

## Design intelligence workflow

### 1. Gather inspiration without copying

Accept screenshots and website references as inspiration.

Use them to understand:
- layout rhythm,
- type personality,
- pacing,
- content hierarchy,
- visual emphasis,
- and how trust is communicated.

Do not copy the exact composition, structure, or copy style. Instead, extract design principles.

### 2. Analyze design language

For every reference, analyze:
- spacing systems,
- typography choices,
- hierarchy and emphasis,
- rhythm across sections,
- visual contrast,
- color psychology,
- CTA placement,
- trust elements,
- section ordering,
- and visual flow.

The design system should be derived from these observations rather than imported wholesale.

### 3. Translate inspiration into an original system

When combining multiple inspirations, create a new synthesis by blending:
- one structural idea,
- one typography approach,
- one pacing principle,
- one trust strategy,
- and one CTA logic.

A strong result comes from combining several influences into one coherent system, not from copying several references at once.

## Design variables to master

### Spacing
Spacing should shape clarity and pace.

Use spacing to:
- create breathing room,
- guide the eye,
- define content hierarchy,
- improve readability,
- and make the page feel deliberate rather than crowded.

### Typography
Typography should communicate tone and confidence.

Consider:
- heading personality,
- body readability,
- contrast between display and body styles,
- alignment and scale,
- and rhythm across paragraphs and sections.

### Hierarchy
Hierarchy must make the page easy to scan.

Every section should clearly communicate:
- what the page is about,
- what action matters most,
- what proof the visitor should trust,
- and where attention should go next.

### Rhythm
Rhythm is the pacing of the page.

It should be shaped by:
- section spacing,
- changes in scale,
- image or media placement,
- and the progression of content from interest to action.

### Color psychology
Color should support intent, not just decoration.

Use color to:
- create trust,
- signal confidence,
- create contrast where needed,
- and support the brand’s real tone.

Avoid arbitrary color palettes that feel disconnected from the business context.

### CTA placement
CTA placement should follow user intent and page flow.

Good CTA placement is informed by:
- the primary conversion goal,
- the visitor’s likely attention path,
- the relationship between sections and action,
- and the need for both primary and secondary options.

### Trust elements
Trust elements should be visible and relevant.

They may include:
- clear service descriptions,
- simple proof points,
- location or service-area context when confirmed,
- contact clarity,
- clear business details,
- and accessible forms or direct communication paths.

Trust should feel earned, not decorative.

### Section ordering
Section ordering should follow a persuasive and understandable sequence.

Typical logic:
1. establish context,
2. communicate value,
3. build trust,
4. guide the next action.

The exact order should adapt to the business and page purpose.

### Visual flow
Visual flow should guide the visitor naturally across the page.

Use layout, scale, contrast, whitespace, and content grouping to create a clear path from introduction to action.

## How to adapt to different industries

The engine must adapt automatically for different industries without becoming generic. Use the Industry Knowledge Engine's suggested tone and emphasis as a starting point for this adaptation, and confirm the final direction against the client's real context rather than applying it automatically.

### Industry adaptation rules
- Home services: emphasize clarity, trust, response time, and direct action.
- Medical or wellness: emphasize clarity, tone, safety, and professionalism.
- Legal services: emphasize authority, credibility, and clear next steps.
- Beauty or personal care: emphasize atmosphere, warmth, and visual polish.
- Contractors and trades: emphasize reliability, scope, and proof of execution.
- Restaurants and hospitality: emphasize experience, atmosphere, and convenience.
- Education and tutoring: emphasize expertise, clarity, and reassurance.

The design should reflect the industry’s expectations while remaining grounded in the client’s real context.

## Design approach by tone

### Minimalism is appropriate when:
- the business is premium or understated,
- the message must feel calm and credible,
- the content is strong and needs breathing room,
- the page should feel focused and editorial,
- and the visitor needs clarity over stimulation.

### Richer designs are better when:
- the business benefits from a stronger sense of experience,
- the brand requires more personality,
- the page needs more emotional resonance,
- the content is visual and exploratory,
- or the business context demands more depth and excitement.

Minimalism and richness are both valid. The decision should be based on audience and brand fit, not default styling.

## Design priorities

Every design must balance:
- conversion,
- accessibility,
- performance,
- SEO,
- Local SEO,
- and AI Visibility.

A premium design is not just visually polished. It must also be:
- easy to use,
- understandable by search engines and AI systems,
- fast to load,
- readable on mobile,
- and clear in its purpose.

## Design review checklist before implementation

Before implementation, review the design plan against the following:

- What is the primary visitor intent?
- What is the primary CTA?
- What is the secondary CTA?
- Which trust elements are required?
- What is the intended section order?
- What is the intended visual flow?
- What spacing and rhythm strategy will be used?
- What typography system will support the tone?
- Which color strategy fits the business context?
- What internal links are required?
- What accessibility needs must be preserved?
- What performance constraints must be respected?
- What SEO and Local SEO requirements apply?
- What structured data is appropriate and supported by visible content?
- What placeholders are still needed?
- What design references were used as inspiration, and how were they transformed rather than copied?

## Quality scorecard

Every completed design should be evaluated with the following scorecard.

### A. Originality
- Does the design feel custom and specific to this client?
- Has the layout been meaningfully transformed rather than reused?

### B. Clarity
- Is the page easy to understand at a glance?
- Is the hierarchy obvious?

### C. Conversion support
- Is the primary CTA obvious?
- Does the page guide the visitor toward a useful action?

### D. Trust
- Are trust elements visible and appropriate?
- Does the page feel credible and grounded?

### E. Accessibility
- Is the design usable with keyboard navigation?
- Are controls, labels, contrast, and headings strong?

### F. Performance
- Does the design remain lightweight and efficient?
- Does it avoid unnecessary complexity?

### G. SEO and discoverability
- Are metadata, heading structure, internal links, and page intent clear?

### H. Local relevance
- Are local details used only when real and approved?

### I. AI visibility
- Is the content structured in a way that is understandable to both humans and AI systems?

## Design guardrails

The system should avoid:
- repeating the same layout pattern for multiple clients,
- relying on decorative filler over real content value,
- using generic card grids as the default solution,
- overloading a page with competing calls to action,
- using visual effects that hurt performance or clarity,
- inventing business details or unsupported claims,
- and letting aesthetics override usability.

## Output expectations

A completed design should feel:
- intentional,
- original,
- credible,
- accessible,
- conversion-aware,
- and production-ready.

It should not feel like a template that could be swapped between any business.
